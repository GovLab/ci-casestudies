CASE STUDY LAST UPDATED: October 2020

Problems such as Mosquito-Borne Diseases (MBDs) require innovative actions. To foster transformation in how governments address health and social issues, the Governance Lab based at New York University (The GovLab) in collaboration with government partners and with the support of the Inter-American Development Bank (IADB) launched “Smarter Crowdsourcing for Zika” in 2016. Smarter Crowdsourcing is a method for tapping the knowledge of worldwide experts to find implementable and innovative solutions to public problems. Typically, policymakers everywhere are facing a crisis of agility - namely the ability to identify, implement and test innovations in response to new challenges quickly.  The Zika crisis created the momentum and the motive to do things differently. The five-step Smarter Crowdsourcing method combines the agility and diversity of crowdsourcing (also called open innovation) with curation to target those with relevant know-how and bring them together in a format designed to produce effective and implementable outcomes.

 

Background

The IADB and The GovLab partnered with the governments of Panama, Colombia, Argentina, and the City of Rio de Janeiro to host a series of online conferences to tap the collective intelligence of local and global experts with relevant skills, experience, and background to come up with innovative and implementable ideas to fight Zika. Instead of organizing a multi-day conference in one physical location which would require participants to arrange for travel and accommodations, and require organizers to secure a venue—all at great expense—these conferences were held online. This reduced overhead, encouraged greater, more diverse and egalitarian participation while reducing climate footprint.

To avoid thinking too big, The GovLab broke down the challenge of mosquito-borne diseases into sixteen smaller, clearer, better defined and more solvable problems.

It partnered with the city government of Rio de Janeiro in Brazil and the national governments of Argentina, Colombia, and Panama to find solutions. Partner governments selected six of the 16 to address: Assessing Public Awareness; Communication and Behavior Change; Predictive Analytics; Trash and Standing Water; Information Collection/Data Governance; and Long-term Care.

For instance, The GovLab started from the intractable issue of Zika to articulate the relatively simple question of how to improve the collection of trash in a given city. At the base of the Zika problem – among other things – a failure to pick up garbage led to an accumulation of standing water and mosquito breeding. Once The GovLab was able to articulate more manageable problems, it could then crowdsource help from global experts to devise solutions such as developing adaptive vehicles for improved pickup, using drones to conduct trash surveillance and working with companies to change popular packaging to reduce the incidence of water accumulation.

 

The result?

The Smarter Crowdsourcing for Zika project brought together a hundred participants in various fields of expertise from six continents to brainstorm the best ideas to combat the Zika virus. These conferences resulted in a total of 20 recommendations grouped under the six problem areas. The Smarter Crowdsourcing team subsequently conducted further research into these 20 recommendations—including identifying costs and benefits, determining the necessary steps and timetables for implementation, and identifying key strategic decisions—with the assurance that the participating governments would prioritize the recommendations they were most interested in pursuing. With this in mind, the endeavor culminated in an Implementation Report designed to enable public authorities to translate these recommendations into practical, implementable improvements in the way they deliver public health policies and services.1

 

How Did It Work?

Between August and October 2016, The GovLab organized six online dialogues over two months in response to each problem - attracting more than 100 global experts, who deliberated online to deepen understanding of the problems and their root causes and to identify solutions. The conferences each lasted two hours and were focused on a particular aspect of the Zika crisis.

These six topics were:

  1. Assessing Public Awareness - What are the most effective ways to capture the public’s understanding of mosquito-borne diseases, and how do we use these findings to inform the design of campaigns to improve public awareness?
  2. Communication and Behavior Change - What are the most effective ways to change public behavior to prevent mosquito breeding and mosquito bites, and expand access to proper healthcare?
  3. Trash and Standing Water - What can be done to eliminate uncollected waste and standing water where mosquitoes breed?
  4. Information Collection and Data Governance - How do we develop better practices and policies for epidemiological data collection and for sharing data with the appropriate authorities?
  5. Long Term Care - How do we provide support to families with members at risk or already suffering from the chronic effects of Zika and other mosquito-borne diseases?
  6. Predictive Analytics - What innovative approaches are available for using data to predict factors influencing outbreaks?

After the conclusion of these conferences, participating governments chose those ideas they were most interested in pursuing.

Figure 1: Topics for the six online conferences which were organized by The GovLab (Source: zika.smartercrowdsourcing.org)

The Smarter Crowdsourcing team subsequently conducted further interviews and research into these ideas, identifying their costs and benefits, the steps required for implementation, and the key strategic decisions that governments would need to make along the way. The team’s findings were then combined into an Implementation Report comprising twenty initiatives selected for their importance and potential impact as well as their suitability for immediate implementation. This Implementation Report was designed to enable public authorities to translate these new ideas into practical improvements in the way they deliver policies and services.

Figure 2: The Smarter Crowdsourcing Zika Report (Source: zika.smartercrowdsourcing.org)

The Smarter Crowdsourcing method comprises five, clearly defined steps. These helped to ensure that the expertise solicited was relevant and useful:

  1. Partner and Problem Identification: The GovLab, IDB and the Latin American government partners identified and prioritized the core problems to address. Each selected topic was the subject of an online conference.
  2. Problem Definition: GovLab briefed each challenge in English, Spanish and Portuguese. Briefings were posted online for public comment. The briefings informed the agenda and discussion for the online dialogues.
  3. Curating Guests: Diverse experts and innovators – from practitioners and technologists to policymakers and researchers – were identified and invited to participate in one or more online conferences, matching their expertise to the topics. Guests were asked for recommendations of additional experts who should participate. The focus was on finding those with innovative yet practical approaches to tackling identified problems.
  4. Online Conferences: Online conferences -- each one of them focused on a specific problem -- brought together experts with partner government officials to brainstorm innovative and practical ways to tackle the problems. Videos, transcripts and post-conference briefings of each discussion to be posted online for comment.
  5. Development of Implementation Plans: The expertise shared during the conferences was integrated into a set of action plans for the government partners. Government partners identified the most viable solutions shared by experts and the GovLab continued working with experts to refine suggestions for implementation.

Figure 3: The Smarter Crowdsourcing methodology (Source: smartercrowdsourcing.org)

 

Why It Worked

Smarter Crowdsourcing was successful at attracting useful ideas from leading global experts in a timely fashion because:

  1. Time-bounded participation: Experts did not have to travel and only needed to participate in the online conference and a follow-up interview.
  2. Government participated together with experts: Relevant officials from partner countries and cities joined the deliberation to answer questions about on-the-ground conditions and make clear that information provided would be used.
  3. Process for going from idea to implementation: The initial work on researching and defining concrete problems combined with expert deliberation and further research helped to produce useful and implementable expertise.
  4. Building a network of experts: The method helps public servants grow a network of knowledgeable and motivated people who are increasingly willing to lend their collective intelligence to difficult issues and reinforces the belief that even a small contribution can make a big difference.
  5. Creating implementation plans: Significant effort was spent on measuring the costs, benefits, timeframes, political will, and overall ease of implementation for each recommendation to increase the chance that proposal would become policy.
