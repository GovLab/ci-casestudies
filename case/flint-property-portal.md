# Flint Neighborhood Inventory / Flint Property Portal

**Data crowdsourced from residents helps Flint secure $US67.5 million to restore blighted neighborhoods**

- Category: Problem Solved
- Topic: Housing
- Location: Flint, Michigan, United States
- Implementation level: city
- Owner: Genesee County Land Bank Authority and the City of Flint
- Years in operation: 2012-present / 2017-present
- Platforms: Website; Mobile app
- Project website: https://flintpropertyportal.com
- Case study last updated: October 2020
- Part of: [Collective Intelligence Case Studies](https://collective-intelligence.thegovlab.org/)

From its peak in 1960, the City of Flint’s population fell by more than 50% by 2018, leaving the city with many vacant homes and empty or unsafe lots. Approximately 21,500 of Flint’s properties, representing 38% of all properties in the city, were considered blighted. Blighted properties are those that are so dilapidated and unsafe that they impede economic growth.[1] Residents identified access to property information and addressing blight as one of their top priorities but local authorities did not have the resources or data to respond effectively.

In 2012, the Genesee County Land Bank Authority - a government organization that manages tax-foreclosed properties - the City of Flint, and the Community Foundation of Greater Flint, partnered to create the Flint Neighborhood Inventory Project. The project involved 27 community-based groups that surveyed the condition of properties in their neighborhoods, enabling the City to obtain existing condition data for a large part of the city and to build relationships with these groups, laying the foundation for a subsequent master planning

community engagement. In turn, that engagement process led to the development of the [Flint Property Portal](https://flintpropertyportal.com/).

Launched in 2017, the Portal displays property data collected by the Land Bank and the City and complements that with data collected from residents. Through the Portal website and mobile app residents can add to the information about a property’s condition. The CIty encourages resident contributions through an annual Neighborhood Inventory program and the Land Bank does the same through a [Clean & Green program](http://www.thelandbank.org/clean_green_prog.asp) that involves the public in maintaining vacant lots. The Portal’s “Maintenance Message” function also enables residents to report their community work, such as boarding, trash removal, and mowing. The City and the Land Bank then use the Portal data to help them make decisions about properties and public services from water pipe replacement to building permits and urban planning.

On the Portal website, residents can also create maps that depict the condition of properties in specific areas or even city-wide, offering them an up-to-date and transparent picture of property conditions.

**Before the Flint Neighborhood Inventory and Flint Property Portal projects, the City and the Land Bank had limited information about blighted properties but using the collective intelligence and collective action of its residents, the Land Bank and the City have created a detailed picture of blight in the city and made it easily accessible to the public.**

**The result?** Through the first Neighborhood Inventory in 2012, residents completed a citywide census of all 56,000 properties in Flint, so that the City and the Land Bank could determine the scale of blight issue and where the specific problems were. The Land Bank, in partnership with the City, then used the data to identify properties in need of demolition as part of a successful application to the US Treasury Hardest Hit Fund that awarded $US67.5 million in blight elimination grants for the Flint area. These grant funds have been used to demolish more than 4,500 blighted structures.[2] In total, users have submitted more than 250,000 messages through the Portal. Of those, 165,000 are from groups participating in the Your Neighborhood Inventory and Clean & Green programs and a further 10,000 are from the general public. The remainder are contributions made by the Land Bank and City staff.

**Perhaps most important, this collaboration between government and citizens has given everyone a transparent picture of conditions in their own city and empowered residents through the process of collaboration in problem-solving.**

Christina Kelly, Director of Planning and Neighborhood Revitalization, Genesee County Land Bank Authority, explains:

Now, more than ever, we need to be transparent. \[The Genesee County Land Bank Authority\] believes the Portal is an effective way to maintain transparency between government and citizens. The stronger the trust between the two, the better they will be able to work together.[3]

**How does it work?**

Consisting of a website and a mobile app, the portal collects, combines and displays 60 points of data for each of Flint’s 56,000 properties. The CIty and the Land Bank selected these data points in response to both the public’s interest and their own data uses.

The Portal offers three main functions:

- Property Lookup: Within the Portal exists a “profile” for every property in the city of Flint, which includes a photograph and up to 40 pieces of property specific information. Anyone with internet access can view this information by entering an address or parcel number or by selecting it using the online map. Residents can easily find out who owns a property, if it is vacant, if it is on a demolition list, and, if it is owned by the Land Bank, when it will be demolished.
- Property Update: Users can report property condition changes through the Property Update feature to keep local officials informed. After selecting a property by inputting the address or parcel number, users can report these changes. All of this information is viewable to the public in a “property feed.” In the app, users are asked to select a land parcel and answer three questions: “What is the property condition?”; “Is the building occupied”; and “Is a blight visit needed?” Then they can upload a photo of the property and add any accompanying text they wish in a text box.
- Map Making: The Portal gives users the ability to map property information. Users can choose to view maps from a list of categories such as demolition status, property condition, and future land use, or they can create their own map using nearly 30 criteria for any area within Flint, small or large.

Through a [partnership](http://www.thelandbank.org/downloads/FlintPropertyPortalCaseStudywGraphics.pdf) between the Community Foundation of Greater Flint and the City of Flint, a Your Neighborhood Inventory initiative is conducted annually to help community groups assess the condition of properties in their neighborhoods. The City identifies inventory needs and engages with participating groups on data collection and improvement plans while The Community Foundation of Greater Flint selects participating groups through an application process and awards a $500 mini-grant to each group that is accepted. The Land Bank’s Clean & Green program similarly involves community groups in maintaining vacant lots and posting maintenance messages and property condition updates to the Portal.

To support the introduction of the Portal, the City and the Land Bank hosted three open house workshops to train people in using the Portal and to get feedback on its design. The cost of developing the Portal totaled approximately $US 220,000.

**Why it Works**

1. **Transparency:** Residents are involved in both collecting and using the data, and any resident can search the data on the Flint Property Portal. As a result it has become a trusted source of information. Developed mainly as a response to the public’s demand for more information, the Portal gives residents straightforward and clear access to public data that they didn’t previously have.
2. **Currency of data:** The Portal makes up-to-date data held by the Land Bank and the City available and easy to use and understand for the public, and provides citizens the opportunity to update the information at any time. The City also updates its data that’s linked to the Portal on a monthly basis, so all datasets and information on the Portal are timely, allowing accurate information to be collected and shared to inform and prioritize effective property remediation.
3. **Community engagement**: The Land Bank and the City have long built a community around the Portal, presenting it to residents and asking for feedback before it was launched in 2017. After listening and revising the Portal based on that feedback, the Land Bank and the City launched the Portal with a series of community launch events held throughout Flint. Afterwards, Land Bank and City staff continued to attend community meetings to share information on the Portal and help residents learn how to download and use the app and website.
