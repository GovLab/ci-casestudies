# The Bristol Approach

**Bristol citizens work with nonprofit arts organisation to collect and analyse air quality data**

- Category: Problem Solved
- Topic: Air pollution
- Location: Bristol, United Kingdom
- Implementation level: city
- Owner: Knowle West Media Centre; Ideas for Change; Bristol City Council
- Years in operation: 2015-present
- Platforms: In-person; mobile app
- Project website: https://www.bristolapproach.org/bristol-approach-projects/air-quality/
- Case study last updated: October 2020
- Part of: [Collective Intelligence Case Studies](https://collective-intelligence.thegovlab.org/)

“Bristol remains a city of challenges,” said Mayor Marvin Rees at Bristol’s [annual State of the City Address](https://www.bristol247.com/news-and-features/news/bristol-remains-city-challenges/) in 2017. But Bristol is responding to its challenges by supporting communities to work with technologists and data ‘experts’ to gather information about on-the-ground conditions, especially air quality.

**In 2015 the city created** [**The Bristol Approach**](https://www.bristolapproach.org/bristol-approach-projects/air-quality/)**, a “citizen sensing” method for running community-driven projects that use sensor technologies and local knowledge to understand and develop solutions to urban issues.** The Approach’s six-step framework allows citizens to work as “sensors” to gather information to address key problems collaboratively, build community and foster relationships between residents and public servants.

**Background**

In 2015 [Knowle West Media Centre](http://kwmc.org.uk/) (KWMC), a nonprofit arts organization that supports people to use technology and the arts to develop creative solutions to challenges, began working with communities in East Bristol to explore how technology could be used to help tackle the issues they were concerned about.

KWMC’s initial pilot was part of the REPLICATE Project, a five-year European funded project exploring how ‘smart’ technology could benefit people and neighbourhoods. The REPLICATE Project in Bristol is led by Bristol City Council. The pilot followed The Bristol Approach - a six-step framework devised by KWMC and the Barcelona-based innovation company Ideas for Change, with support from the Bristol City Council.

In the pilot, KWMC worked with artists to hold a series of informal, creative face-to-face conversations with residents in South Bristol and East Bristol at popular “hotspots” such as community spaces, nail salons, and cafes to identify issues people cared about, eventually settling on damp homes as the issue that generated the greatest interest from participants.[1]

KWMC then supported residents to develop a data collection system, where volunteers used sensors -- hardware co-created at KWMC workshops with artists and others from the community -- to measure dampness in their homes by recording temperature and humidity data. The findings were then presented to the City Council, along with proposed measures on how landlords and tenants could work together to improve the situation. The entire pilot process took approximately a year and a half.[2]

Learning from this first pilot informed a second pilot in East Bristol as part of the REPLICATE Project, beginning in 2016 and focusing on the Ashley, Easton and Lawrence Hill wards.[3] Certain areas experienced heavy traffic flow and residents had voiced concerns about air quality and a lack of awareness of the impact that air pollution was having on their health.

**The result?**

From 2016 to date (February 2020) KWMC engaged over 850 people with the process of ’citizen sensing’. In both the damp homes and air quality pilot projects, co-creation design sessions produced novel sensor designs embraced by participants. Six families tested the “ladybird” air quality sensor and accompanying mobile app. The portable “ladybird” air quality sensor could be attached to bicycles, bags, and car windscreens in order to gather nitrogen dioxide data. Academics from the University of the West of England (UWE) Computer Science department worked to develop hardware and software that people could use on their phones to connect to these physical mobile sensors. Thirteen community buildings in east Bristol were also fitted with air quality sensors, built using inexpensive [Luftdaten](https://luftdaten.info/en/home-en/) sensor kits. As the air quality project was completed only recently, the data produced has yet to prompt any decisive government action, but the city council and KWMC are actively considering its potential uses.

In the meantime, select community members are using the learnings to inspire real change, seeking out “cleaner” travel routes or changing their own travel behavior, with some even giving away their cars. In one particularly illustrative case, Sioban (a pseudonym), learned of the project on social media and wanted to get involved so she began to collect data using the ladybird sensor. In 2019, she attended a ‘Making sense of your data’ workshop with KWMC to learn how to interpret the data she gathered. Initially Sioban wanted to get involved when she noticed that she had developed breathing issues after changing her daily cycling commute from the countryside to the center of Bristol. When the doctors told her that the level of pollution in her lungs from inhaling poor quality air in the neighborhood she cycled through was equivalent to smoking two cigarettes daily, she changed her route and saw her breathing improve.

Realizing that topography and minor route changes can seriously influence air quality and its effect on your health, Sioban wanted to gather data to improve the quality of urban life around Bristol. “As an architect, I can use that information when I am designing spaces and actively respond to how people are experiencing and perceiving public spaces,” said Sioban, “and hopefully that would improve the public spaces.”[4]

The Bristol Approach has also been used by young adults  to collect data surrounding other issues, including mental health and food waste.

**How does it work?**

KWMC is the Bristol Living Lab and a member of the [European Network of Living Labs](https://enoll.org/). As the Bristol Living Lab, KWMC provides support and a space where people from different backgrounds can come together to innovate, experiment and address social challenges.

KWMC uses an ‘action research’ process – a specific approach to research that does not start with a fixed ‘hypothesis’ to test but with an idea that could be explored further. The Bristol Approach framework is inspired by KWMC’s practice: rather than ‘pushing’ technology or pre-determined ‘solutions’ onto people, KWMC works with local people, artists, technologists and other partners to ‘pull in’ the resources needed to develop ideas and tools for tackling local challenges and creating new opportunities.

The air quality pilot followed the six steps of the Bristol Approach:

**Step 1: Identification (March 2017 - July 2017)** — KWMC held several in-person events in the Ashley, Easton, Lawrence Hill areas of east Bristol where citizens and organizations could discuss community issues. These events included workshops, community meetings, one-on-one conversations, and working in community venues.

**Step 2: Framing (September 2017 - October 2017)** —  Several issues related to air quality emerged as a particular concern for five groups: cyclists, schoolchildren/parents, taxi drivers, social housing tenants, and community venues.[5] The issues included a lack of knowledge of local air quality, concerns about links between air quality and health, and fumes created by idling cars.  KWMC acknowledged how the lack of information and data about air quality in Bristol prevented the community from developing measures to mitigate the debilitating health effects.

**Step 3: Design (November 2017 - January 2018)** — KWMC then led issue-based design and making sessions with each of the groups to “identify the problem they wanted to explore in more detail, the data they wanted to collect, and how to go about it.” The groups developed ideas for how to design and implement air quality sensors to collect data on-the-go, and worked with an artist to design prototypes (shaped like a ladybird) which were created at KWMC’s digital manufacturing space, KWMC: The Factory. The groups also deliberated on how the data would be stored and shared. They worked with KWMC and [Data Unity](https://www.watershed.co.uk/studio/projects/data-unity) to establish a privacy and data sharing agreement that was then used on the mobile app that was linked to the ladybird sensors.

**Step 4: Deployment (March 2018 - February 2019)** — Six of the portable ladybird citizens were deployed and tested by citizens, who were encouraged to act as “human sensors,” recording their movements and experiences to provide context for the statistical data.[6] Thirteen stationary Luftdaten sensors were also placed in community buildings and spaces. During this period, KWMC also held events such as “Women, Bikes & Bristol” to engage groups that aren’t typically heard when it comes to “smart technology” initiatives.

**Step 5: Orchestration (March 2019 onwards)** — In March 2019, KWMC ran three workshops to teach 21 Bristol residents to analyze the data they were collecting from the sensors.[7] Once it was time to visualize the data, KWMC digital fabrication experts worked with participants to design ways to tell their data stories to the community in-person and online. For example, one group made a “travel air gauge” based on a see-saw in order to encourage residents to base their transport choices on balancing the scale of their choices’ environmental impact.

**Step 6: Outcome (March 2019 onwards)** — KWMC and the participants worked with digital artists and technologists to design visualizations of the air quality data that was gathered at one of the community spaces—a primary school in Easton. The artist used different colors to represent levels of nitrogen dioxide, ozone, and particulate matter observed every 15 minutes over 28 days. These visualizations were placed on postcards and distributed around east Bristol, and other participating spaces could view the data on an online open source platform developed by Data Unity. As an arts organization, KWMC emphasizes the importance of presenting information in a way that is visually appealing and easier to understand than complicated charts.[8]

Employing artists to work on the project is a key aspect that sets the Bristol Approach apart from traditional research projects and studies. According to Carolyn Hassan, founder and director of Knowle West Media Centre, the artists were able to create more fun, expressive, and engaging ways to work with people throughout the project. This helped them dig deeper to the root of the city’s issues and citizen’s concerns, and design and engagement in which citizens felt more inclined to contribute. KWMC views citizen sensing as “a process where citizens build, use, or act as sensors to gather information that will help them to tackle an issue that’s important to them.”[9] Thus, the goal of this project was to help people in Bristol use digital tools to learn more about their environment and explore new ways of sensing their surroundings.

This process of designing sensors and gathering and analyzing air quality data concludes phase one of the air quality pilot. Though the precise details of what phase two entails have yet to be finalized and publicized, it is likely going to explore questions such as: how can citizens combine air quality data with data on other types of pollution such as noise or other quality data such as water, how they can implement multiple types of sensors such as primitive or complex designs, and how they can be tech-agnostic to adapt to different situations and continue to work efficiently with citizens.

**Why It Works**

> *“If there is one thing I would underline about the Bristol Approach, it’s that it is a work in progress. We are still learning and still gathering data as we engage with communities and build trust, all to the end of improving the approach’s effectiveness. In order to learn more and grow, you need to build trust and deepen the engagement with communities” — Carolyn Hassan, founder and director, Knowle West Media Centre*

1. **Collaboration:** Citizens, developers, community organizations, researchers, technologists, and more, are involved in the design and testing of products and processes. Experts worked with citizens directly in events, workshops, and more, to train them in data collection and guide their outputs while ensuring they feel valued and their local expertise is recognised.
2. **Equal emphasis on data and people:** When collected, stored, and analyzed properly, data can be a valuable resource for administrations and general problem-solvers to leverage for informing and solving crucial issues - and also for communities wanting to understand a particular problem. However, data is not useful if it does not ultimately serve people. Hence the minds behind the Bristol Approach took care to ensure the data being collected was addressing the issues people truly cared about, and that human behavior was considered just as heavily as technology.
3. **Collective action and use of latent resources:** Now, Bristol’s local administration has key data on air pollution. This data combined with other community resources such as expertise, technology, and relationships can all be used for future efforts. The social issues explored in the problem identification phase that do not advance to the next stage are placed in an open access repository to be used by other interested parties in the future.[10] Equipped with this new information, government, businesses, and residents can all work together to drive change toward cleaner air and solve related public problems.
